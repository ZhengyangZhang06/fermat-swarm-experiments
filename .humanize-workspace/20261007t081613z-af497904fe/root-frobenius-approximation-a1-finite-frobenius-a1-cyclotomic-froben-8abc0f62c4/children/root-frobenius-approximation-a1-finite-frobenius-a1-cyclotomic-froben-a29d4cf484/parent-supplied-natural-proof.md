## Proof

1. We first establish the finite arithmetic facts used in the argument. All finite fields of characteristic zero occurring below are number fields inside Ω. Their finite extensions are separable, since an irreducible characteristic-zero polynomial is relatively prime to its nonzero derivative. Extending an embedding across each algebraic generator by choosing a root of its transported minimal polynomial gives as many embeddings as the extension degree. Finite extensions are simple: for two generators α,β, distinct embeddings can agree on α+rβ for at most one rational r unless their β-images agree, in which case they never agree there. Avoid finitely many exceptional r; the resulting element has as many distinct images as the extension degree and therefore generates the extension. Induction treats a finite generating family. Scaling clears denominators to give an integral primitive element over ℚ. If K/F is finite Galois and H ≤ Gal(K/F), a primitive element α has |H| distinct H-translates. Its orbit polynomial has coefficients in K^H, while all these translates are roots of its minimal polynomial over K^H. The two degree inequalities prove [K:K^H] = |H|. For any intermediate field K′, normality and separability give [K:K′] automorphisms over K′, so their fixed field is K′ by this degree equality. Extending embeddings and restricting them then proves the fixed-field correspondence and the normal-subgroup quotient assertion.
2. Every finite multiplicative subgroup of a field is cyclic. Let d be the least common multiple of its element orders. Taking suitable powers supplies an element of each maximal prime-power order dividing d. Their product has order d: powers of two commuting factors of coprime orders can cancel only when both are one, because the intersection of their cyclic subgroups is trivial. Every group element is a root of X^d−1, so the polynomial root bound gives at most d elements, while the constructed element has d distinct powers. The root bound itself follows by division by X−a and induction on degree. In particular, for N > 0 the Nth roots of unity in Ω form a cyclic group of order N, since X^N−1 splits and has distinct roots. This argument also proves cyclicity of the multiplicative group of a finite field.
3. The integer ring O_K of a degree-d number field K is free of rank d over ℤ, Noetherian, integrally closed, and has fraction field K. Finitely many integral elements generate a finite module algebra, because their monic equations reduce powers. Conversely, if multiplication by x preserves a nonzero finite module in a field, choose module generators and a matrix of multiplication relations. The adjugate identity for xI minus that matrix makes its monic determinant annihilate the module, and hence vanish in the field. These arguments prove closure of integral elements under sums and products and transitivity of integrality. Scale a rational basis of K to integral elements. The trace pairing is nondegenerate: on a primitive-element power basis its matrix is the transpose of the conjugate-evaluation matrix times that matrix, whose Vandermonde determinant is nonzero. Evaluation at conjugates diagonalizes multiplication, so trace is the sum of conjugates. Traces of integral elements are rational integers; a reduced rational fraction satisfying a monic integer equation has denominator one. Therefore O_K lies between the lattice spanned by the scaled basis and its trace-dual lattice. A subgroup of ℤ^d is finite free: project to a coordinate, lift a generator of its cyclic image, split off this summand, and induct on the kernel. The lattice bounds give rank d. Ideals are such subgroups and their ℤ-generators also generate them as ideals, proving Noetherianity. Transitivity gives integral closedness; denominator clearing gives the fraction-field assertion.
4. Every nonzero prime 𝔮 of O_K is maximal with finite residue field, and (O_K)_𝔮 is a discrete valuation ring. For nonzero a ∈ 𝔮, its monic integral minimal polynomial has nonzero constant term, which belongs to 𝔮. Thus 𝔮 contains a rational prime ℓ. The quotient is a finite domain and hence a field. Its localization A is a one-dimensional Noetherian local integrally closed domain. Integral closedness localizes because multiplying a root of a monic equation over A by a common denominator outside 𝔮 makes it integral over O_K. To obtain a uniformizer, let 𝔞 be its nonzero maximal ideal and choose nonzero a ∈ 𝔞. The only prime containing (a) is 𝔞, so √(a) = 𝔞. The radical criterion follows by localizing at powers of an element outside the radical and contracting a maximal ideal. Finite generation of 𝔞 gives a least n ≥ 1 with 𝔞^n ⊆ (a). If n = 1, then 𝔞 = (a). Otherwise choose b ∈ 𝔞^(n−1) outside (a) and put x = b/a. Then x𝔞 ⊆ A but x ∉ A. If x𝔞 ⊆ 𝔞, the determinant argument of step 3 on the finite nonzero module 𝔞 makes x integral, a contradiction. Hence xy is a unit for some y ∈ 𝔞. For every z ∈ 𝔞, z/y = (xz)/(xy) ∈ A, so 𝔞 = (y). A nonzero element cannot belong to all (y^j): otherwise the ascending principal ideals generated by its quotients by y^j stabilize, and cancellation forces y to be a unit. Every nonzero element is therefore a largest power of y times a unit. Differences of these exponents define the discrete valuation on the fraction field.
5. Nonzero fractional ideals of O_K factor uniquely into prime ideals with integer exponents. For an integral ideal I, take the minimum local valuation of its finite generators at each prime. Only finitely many minima are positive: for nonzero a ∈ I, multiplication by a on an integral basis has nonzero integer determinant, so O_K/(a) is finite and has only finitely many primes. The prescribed product of prime powers has the same localization as I at every maximal ideal. Localizations detect membership and equality: if x ∉ J, a maximal ideal containing the proper ideal {r : rx ∈ J} detects this failure after localization. For inversion, I⁻¹ = {x ∈ K : xI ⊆ O_K} is finite, since multiplication by a nonzero element of I embeds it into O_K. Clearing denominators on finite generators shows that inversion commutes with localization. Thus II⁻¹ is locally, and therefore globally, the unit ideal. Denominator clearing extends factorization to fractional ideals, and local valuations give uniqueness.
6. For an integral ideal I define N(I) = |O_K/I|. Norms multiply and N((α)) = |N_(K/ℚ)(α)|; extend them multiplicatively to fractional ideals. To prove multiplicativity, distinct prime powers are comaximal: expand a sufficiently high power of a+b = 1 with a,b in the distinct primes. Chinese remaindering separates their quotients. At one prime, localization does not change the successive prime-power quotients, and a local uniformizer identifies each quotient with a one-dimensional residue-field space. Counting gives N(𝔮^e) = N(𝔮)^e. For principal ideals, integer row and column reductions identify lattice index with absolute determinant of multiplication by α; denominators handle nonintegral α. Only finitely many integral ideals have bounded norm: an ideal of norm n contains nO_K and corresponds to a subgroup of the finite additive group O_K/nO_K.
7. At most [K:ℚ] primes of K lie over a rational prime ℓ. Chinese remaindering gives a quotient of O_K/ℓO_K whose dimension is the sum of their positive residue degrees. Every nonzero prime of a number field F has a prime above it in a finite extension K. Indeed, localize the integral extension of integer rings away from the lower prime and take a maximal ideal upstairs. Its contraction is maximal: if a field is integral over a domain, a monic equation for the inverse of a nonzero element expresses that inverse in the domain, so the domain is a field. The local base ring has the specified unique maximal ideal.
8. In a finite Galois extension K/F with group J, J acts transitively on primes above a fixed lower prime. Otherwise Chinese remaindering supplies an integral a reducing to zero on one orbit and one on another. The product of all J-translates of a belongs to O_F, and has residues zero and one above the same lower prime, a contradiction.
9. The decomposition group J_𝔔 surjects onto the automorphism group of the residue extension. To identify the fixed elements of its image, suppose b̄ is fixed by that image. Choose integral a reducing to b̄ at 𝔔 and to zero at every other prime over the lower prime. The polynomial ∏_(h∈J)(T−h(a)) belongs to O_F[T] and reduces at 𝔔 to T^(|J|−|J_𝔔|)(T−b̄)^|J_𝔔|. Write |J_𝔔| = r^v u, with r the residue characteristic and r ∤ u. The coefficient of degree |J|−r^v is −u b̄^(r^v), so b̄^(r^v) belongs to the lower residue field. The r^v-power map is bijective on that finite field and injective upstairs, giving b̄ in the lower field. If the residue sizes are Q and Q^f, the Q-power automorphism has order f: its f-th power is the identity, and the polynomial root bound excludes a smaller positive power. There are at most f automorphisms because a generator of the upper multiplicative group generates the field and has minimal-polynomial degree f. Thus this automorphism group is cyclic of order f. A proper subgroup is generated by a d-th power of Frobenius for a divisor d > 1 of f; it fixes the Q^d roots of X^(Q^d)−X. These roots lie upstairs because this polynomial divides X^(Q^f)−X, and it has distinct roots. Its fixed field would therefore be strictly larger than the lower field, contradicting the fixed-element conclusion. Surjectivity follows.
10. For every finite extension K/F and nonzero lower prime 𝔮, [K:F] = Σ_(𝔔|𝔮) e_𝔔 f_𝔔. Localizing O_K over (O_F)_𝔮 gives a finite torsion-free module of rank [K:F] over a discrete valuation ring. It is free: project its fraction vector space to a nonzero coordinate; the finite image is a principal fractional ideal; lift a generator, split off that summand and induct on rank. Reduction modulo 𝔮, prime-power filtrations and Chinese remaindering give the degree formula. In the Galois case transitivity makes e,f constant, and orbit–stabilizer gives |J_𝔔| = ef. Step 9 then gives inertia order e. At an unramified prime there is a unique decomposition-group element inducing arithmetic residue Frobenius, of order f. Conjugation of primes conjugates this element, and restriction to Galois subextensions preserves it. Normalized local valuations also show that ramification indices multiply in towers.
11. Only finitely many rational primes ramify in a finite rational Galois extension K. Choose an integral primitive element. Its nonzero polynomial discriminant is an integer. Outside its prime divisors the distinct conjugates have distinct reductions, since their squared differences multiply to that discriminant. An inertia element preserves reduction, hence fixes the primitive element and is the identity. Step 10 gives unramifiedness. Every finite collection of number fields is contained in a finite rational Galois extension: adjoin all roots of the finitely many minimal polynomials of generators. Adjoining algebraic elements gives finiteness, embeddings permute these roots, and characteristic zero gives separability. Passing to such a container and using multiplicativity of ramification indices proves finiteness of ramification for general number-field extensions.
12. For a number field K, ζ_K(s) = Σ_(I≠0) N(I)^−s converges absolutely for Re(s) > 1 and equals its ideal Euler product. Step 7 bounds the prime-ideal sum by [K:ℚ] times Σ_ℓ ℓ^−Re(s), which is bounded by the convergent integer series. After removing finitely many factors, the inequality −log(1−x) ≤ 2x for 0 ≤ x ≤ 1/2 proves product convergence. Expanding finite products and using unique ideal factorization proves the series identity. Bounds using Re(s) are uniform on compact subsets of this half-plane. The next steps prove the pole and its ray-class refinement independently of prime distribution.
13. For a number field F put d = [F:ℚ] = r₁+2r₂ and W = ℝ^r₁ × ℂ^r₂. Factoring a primitive polynomial over ℝ and applying polynomial Chinese remaindering identifies F⊗ℚℝ with W. The complex splitting result used here is the fundamental theorem of algebra, specifically Complex.exists_root in the pinned Mathlib/Analysis/Complex/Polynomial/Basic.lean:35–53, also publicly identified by https://github.com/leanprover-community/mathlib4/blob/db584cd6d46c92f209a44c0f1c829460d327499d/Mathlib/Analysis/Complex/Polynomial/Basic.lean#L35. Integrating the number of translates of a bounded measurable set over a lattice fundamental parallelepiped shows that volume larger than the covolume forces two distinct points to differ by a lattice vector. Apply this to half a symmetric convex body: volume larger than 2^d times the covolume forces a nonzero lattice point in the body.
14. The ordinary ideal class group is finite. Apply step 13 to an integral ideal 𝔞 and a product of intervals and disks of radii c N(𝔞)^(1/d), with c fixed sufficiently large. It supplies nonzero α ∈ 𝔞 with |N_(F/ℚ)(α)| ≤ c^d N(𝔞). The absolute norm is the product of the absolute real coordinates and squared complex moduli, by diagonalizing multiplication through the embeddings. The integral ideal (α)𝔞⁻¹ represents the inverse class and has bounded norm. Step 6 gives finitely many possibilities. Every fractional ideal class has an integral representative after clearing denominators, proving the assertion.
15. Unit logarithms form a full lattice. Give real places weight e_v = 1 and complex places weight e_v = 2, and put H₀ = {x : Σ e_v x_v = 0}. For a unit u, λ(u) = (log|u|_v) lies in H₀ because its absolute norm is one. Bounded logarithms bound all embeddings, and a lattice has finitely many points in a bounded set. Thus the logarithmic image Λ is discrete and its kernel is finite. That kernel consists exactly of roots of unity: roots have zero logarithms, and every element of a finite multiplicative group has finite order. For x ∈ H₀ use step 13 with radii c exp(x_v), whose weighted product is fixed. It supplies nonzero integral α with log|α|_v−x_v uniformly bounded above. Their weighted sum is log|N(α)| ≥ 0, which also bounds each difference below. Moreover |N(α)| is bounded, so only finitely many ideals (α) occur. Choose generators β_j for these ideals; α/β_j is a unit whose logarithm stays within a fixed distance of x. Hence Λ spans H₀. Choose a real basis from Λ and let Λ₀ be its integer span. A bounded fundamental parallelepiped contains representatives for Λ/Λ₀, and bounded sets contain only finitely many points of Λ. Thus this quotient is finite, say of order k, and kΛ ⊆ Λ₀. The subgroup argument for ℤ^r from step 3 gives a lattice basis for Λ. This also covers dimension zero.
16. Let a modulus 𝔣 consist of a nonzero integral ideal 𝔣₀ and specified real places. Its ray class group consists of fractional ideals supported away from 𝔣₀ modulo principal ideals generated by elements congruent to one at its finite primes and positive at its specified real places. It is finite. Forgetting these conditions maps it to the finite ordinary class group. The kernel is a quotient of elements having valuation zero at the modulus primes, modulo their residue and sign conditions. These residues and signs belong to the finite group (O_F/𝔣₀)ˣ × {±1}^𝔣∞. Denominators prime to the modulus exist by Chinese remaindering, and cross-multiplication proves independence of the resulting residue. The kernel is consequently finite. Every ray class has an integral representative prime to 𝔣₀: choose a denominator-clearing element congruent to one modulo 𝔣₀, and add a sufficiently large positive multiple of a positive rational integer lying in both the denominator ideal and 𝔣₀. This preserves denominator clearing and congruence and ensures all required positive signs.
17. Fix an integral representative 𝔞 of a ray class C, prime to 𝔣₀. Its nonzero integral ideals prime to 𝔣₀ are exactly α𝔞 with nonzero α ∈ 1+𝔣₀𝔞⁻¹ and the prescribed signs. At modulus primes this condition is the ray congruence because 𝔞 is locally the unit ideal. Elsewhere it is α ∈ 𝔞⁻¹, since 𝔣₀ is locally the unit ideal and 1 ∈ 𝔞⁻¹. Localization detects the global equivalence. Two parameters give the same ideal exactly when their quotient is a ray unit. The ray-unit group U_𝔣 has finite index in O_Fˣ by the finite residue-and-sign map. Its logarithmic image Λ_𝔣 is therefore a full lattice in H₀, with finite logarithmic kernel of some order w_𝔣 ≥ 1.
18. Choose a half-open fundamental parallelepiped B₁ for Λ_𝔣. For z ∈ W with nonzero coordinates put r = |N(z)|^(1/d) and b_v = log|z_v|−log r. Let ℛ consist of 0 < r ≤ 1, b ∈ B₁, the prescribed positive real signs, all choices of remaining real signs and all complex arguments. It is bounded and has positive volume: the radial, logarithmic and angular parameters give local coordinates with nonsingular derivatives in the interior. Its boundary is covered by finitely many Lipschitz images of bounded (d−1)-dimensional boxes and the origin. Indeed, the formulas ±r exp(b_v) and r exp(b_v+iθ_v) have bounded derivatives on closed parameter boxes, whose faces cover the boundary away from the origin. Each admissible ray-unit orbit has exactly w_𝔣 representatives whose logarithmic component is in B₁: there is a unique logarithmic lattice translate into B₁, and the logarithmic kernel acts freely on nonzero elements, preserving the sign conditions and r.
19. For any full lattice Λ, any translate z₀+Λ and t ≥ 1, the number of its points in tℛ is vol(ℛ)t^d/covol(Λ) + O(t^(d−1)). Tile W by bounded fundamental cells anchored at the translated lattice points. Cells whose closures avoid the boundary contribute equally to the point count and normalized volume. Subdivide the boundary parameter boxes into O(t^(d−1)) boxes of side at most 1/t. After dilation each image has bounded diameter and meets a uniformly bounded number of lattice cells, by the positive minimum separation of lattice points and the bounded cell diameter. Only O(t^(d−1)) cells can contribute error. The origin and half-open faces obey the same bound; when d = 1 it is O(1).
20. Apply step 19 to 1+𝔣₀𝔞⁻¹ and divide by w_𝔣. The norm bound N(α𝔞) ≤ X corresponds to t = (X/N(𝔞))^(1/d). Since covol(𝔣₀𝔞⁻¹) = Δ_F N(𝔣₀)/N(𝔞), the number A_C(X) of these ideals is κ_𝔣 X + O(X^(1−1/d)), where κ_𝔣 = vol(ℛ)/(w_𝔣 Δ_F N(𝔣₀)) > 0 is independent of C. Enlarging the error constant covers bounded X, t < 1 and exclusion of the zero parameter.
21. Summation by parts gives ζ_C(s) = s∫₁∞ A_C(t)t^(−s−1)dt for Re(s) > 1. The main term is κ_𝔣 s/(s−1). The error integral is holomorphic for Re(s) > 1−1/d: on compact subsets its differentiated integrands are bounded by integrable multiples of t^(−1−ε)(1+log t)^j. Uniform convergence of truncated integrals and their derivatives justifies differentiation. Thus ζ_C(s) = κ_𝔣/(s−1) plus a function holomorphic near one. For the trivial modulus, summing over the finitely many ideal classes proves that ζ_F has a positive simple-pole limit along real s decreasing to one. This derivation has used no Frobenius distribution or reciprocity.
22. Now let q be a rational prime and K = F(ζ_q), where ζ_q is primitive. This is a finite Galois extension, the splitting field of X^q−1. Its group J embeds in (ℤ/qℤ)ˣ by its exponent action, so is cyclic by step 2. Away from q the qth roots are units in the upper valuation ring, and reduction on them is injective. Unit membership follows from the valuation property and ζ^q = 1. If a nonidentity root ξ reduces to one, cancel ξ−1 in ξ^q−1 and reduce the resulting geometric sum; this would give q = 0 in the residue field. Hence inertia fixes ζ_q and is trivial away from q. Arithmetic Frobenius at a prime 𝔭 of F acts on ζ_q with exponent N(𝔭), because its residue action does so and reduction is injective.
23. Let T be any finite set of primes of F containing those above q. The free abelian group of fractional ideals supported outside T admits a homomorphism Φ_T to J, sending each prime to its Frobenius. Under J → (ℤ/qℤ)ˣ, its value is the positive rational ideal norm reduced modulo q; its numerator and denominator are prime to q. Set 𝔣₀ = qO_F times the product of the primes in T, and include all real places in the modulus. For α satisfying the ray conditions, the denominator ideal I = {x ∈ O_F : xα ∈ O_F} has no factor above q. Thus b = N(I) is prime to q, bO_F ⊆ I and bα is integral. At primes above q, α−1 ∈ qO_F locally, so bα−b belongs locally to qO_F. At all other primes q is a unit and bα−b is integral. Localization therefore gives bα−b ∈ qO_F globally.
24. On an integral basis, multiplication by bα is congruent modulo q to b times the identity. Taking determinants gives N_(F/ℚ)(bα) ≡ b^[F:ℚ] modulo q. Divide by the invertible denominator to obtain N_(F/ℚ)(α) ≡ 1 modulo q. This norm is positive because all its real factors are positive and the nonreal factors occur in conjugate pairs. Thus N((α)) also has residue one. Injectivity of J → (ℤ/qℤ)ˣ shows Φ_T((α)) = 1, so Φ_T descends to a homomorphism Art from the ray class group to J.
25. The homomorphism Art is surjective. Let H be its image and F′ = K^H. Since J is abelian, F′/F is Galois. Every prime outside T is unramified and has trivial Frobenius in F′, so splits into d′ = [F′:F] primes of residue degree one. Euler products give ζ_(F′), with factors above T removed, equal to the d′th power of ζ_F with factors in T removed. By step 21 both have positive simple-pole asymptotics, and the finitely many removed factors have positive nonzero limits. If d′ > 1, multiplication by (s−1)^d′ makes the first side tend to zero and the other to a positive number. Therefore d′ = 1 and H = J.
26. For a complex character ψ of J define L_T(s,ψ) = ∏_(𝔭∉T)(1−ψ(Frob_𝔭)N(𝔭)^−s)⁻¹ when Re(s) > 1. Its sum over the ray class group is zero: translation by a class where θ differs from one preserves the sum while multiplying it by a scalar different from one. Every nontrivial L_T(s,ψ) is consequently holomorphic near one.
27. Put m = |J|. At a prime outside T whose Frobenius has order f, the m characters take every fth root of unity exactly m/f times. Hence ∏_ψ(1−ψ(Frob_𝔭)X) = (1−X^f)^(m/f). Step 10 gives m/f upper primes, each of norm N(𝔭)^f. Multiplying these local identities proves that ζ_K with factors above T removed equals ∏_ψ L_T(s,ψ). Its trivial-character factor is ζ_F with its T-factors removed. The quotient by this factor has a positive nonzero limit at one, by step 21. All remaining factors have finite limits by step 26, so none can vanish at one. If m = 1 there are no nontrivial factors.
28. As real s decreases to one, Σ_(𝔭∉T) ψ(Frob_𝔭)N(𝔭)^−s = δ_(ψ,1) log(1/(s−1)) + O(1). Its difference from the Euler logarithm consists of terms of exponent at least two and is bounded by 2Σ_𝔭 N(𝔭)^−2. For the trivial character, the positive simple pole gives the stated logarithm. For a nontrivial character, divide L_T by its nonzero value at one and shrink a neighborhood until the quotient is within distance one of 1. The logarithm power series supplies a bounded logarithm there. On a connected real interval it differs from the Euler logarithm by a constant multiple of 2πi, since their exponentials agree and their difference is continuous. This proves the formula.
29. For each a ∈ J, character orthogonality gives Σ_(𝔭∉T,Frob_𝔭=a) N(𝔭)^−s = m⁻¹ log(1/(s−1)) + O(1). Indeed, m⁻¹Σ_ψ conjugate(ψ(a))ψ(b) is the indicator of a = b, by the finite geometric-sum identity in a cyclic group. Primes of rational residue degree at least two have norms at least ℓ², with at most [F:ℚ] primes above each ℓ. Thus infinitely many rational-residue-degree-one primes of F have any prescribed Frobenius a in F(ζ_q)/F, even after additional finite exclusions.
30. For every m ≥ 1 and finite set B₁ of rational primes, there is a prime q outside B₁ with m dividing q−1. For m > 1 define Φ_m by the primitive mth roots. The identities X^n−1 = ∏_(d|n) Φ_d(X), induction and division by monic integer polynomials prove Φ_m ∈ ℤ[X]; evaluating at zero gives Φ_m(0) = 1 for m > 1. Its degree is positive by step 2. Choose large positive t divisible by m and every prime in B₁ with |Φ_m(t)| > 1. A prime divisor q of Φ_m(t) divides neither t nor m and lies outside B₁, since Φ_m(t) is one modulo every prime divisor of t. In characteristic q, X^m−1 is squarefree. Its cyclotomic factors have disjoint roots. The residue of t, being a root of Φ_m, cannot have a proper-divisor order, for then it would also be a root of another cyclotomic factor. Its order is therefore m in (ℤ/qℤ)ˣ, proving m ∣ q−1. For m = 1, take a prime divisor of 1+k∏_(r∈B₁)r with k chosen so this integer exceeds one.
31. For a rational prime q and primitive ζ_q, C = ℚ(ζ_q) has degree q−1 and Galois group (ℤ/qℤ)ˣ, and q is totally ramified in every intermediate field. Indeed, Φ_q(X) = 1+X+⋯+X^(q−1), and Φ_q(X+1) is Eisenstein at q. To justify irreducibility, products of primitive integer polynomials are primitive by reduction modulo primes, so a monic rational factorization can be made monic integral. Modulo q both nonconstant factors of the shifted polynomial would be powers of X, forcing both constant terms divisible by q although their product is q. This contradiction proves irreducibility, and the q−1 conjugates give the asserted Galois group. The norm of 1−ζ_q is Φ_q(1) = q. For 1 ≤ i < q, (1−ζ_q^i)/(1−ζ_q) is integral by geometric summation and has norm one because numerator and denominator are conjugate. Its integral multiplication matrix has determinant one, so its adjugate gives an integral inverse. These quotients are units. Their product gives qO_C = (1−ζ_q)^(q−1) as ideals. The ideal (1−ζ_q) has norm q and is the unique prime above q, with residue degree one. Prime lifting and residue inclusion give a unique prime of residue degree one in each intermediate field. The degree formula then makes its ramification index equal to that intermediate field's degree over ℚ.
32. Return to the given E and σ. Normality makes g = σ|E an element of Gal(E/ℚ); let m be its order. By steps 11 and 30 choose a prime q > 2, unramified in E, with m ∣ q−1. Put C = ℚ(ζ_q). The intersection E∩C is unramified at q as a subfield of E and totally ramified at q as a subfield of C. Multiplicativity of ramification indices and step 31 force [E∩C:ℚ] = 1. Put M = EC. Restriction is injective because E and C generate M, so [M:C] = [E:ℚ]. Restriction to the two factors is now an injective map between finite groups of equal size and identifies Gal(M/ℚ) with Gal(E/ℚ) × Gal(C/ℚ).
33. Choose a ∈ Gal(C/ℚ) of order m, possible by cyclicity and m ∣ q−1. Put h = (g,a), H = ⟨h⟩ and F = M^H. Then M/F is cyclic of degree m. 34. Let T contain the primes of F above q, above rational primes in B, and above prime divisors of the nonzero discriminant of an integral primitive element of M.

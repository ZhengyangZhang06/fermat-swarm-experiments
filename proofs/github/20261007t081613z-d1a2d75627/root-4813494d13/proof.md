## Statement, assumptions, and proof boundary

Put `K = AlgebraicClosure ℚ`, let `E` be the base change to `K` of the given
integer Weierstrass equation `W`, and put `V = E[p]`, realized by the frozen
`Submodule.torsionBy ℤ ... p`. Let `I = A.inertiaSubgroupIn ℚ`. We must construct
a proper `ZMod p` submodule `L` of this exact module such that `σv − v ∈ L` for
every `σ ∈ I` and `v ∈ V`, with the action given by the frozen
`galoisRepModuleEnd`.

The assumptions are: `p` is prime and different from 2; `W.Δ ≠ 0`;
`W.IsSemistableModel`; either `p` divides `W.Δ` or `W.preΨ' p` has an integer
coefficient prime to `p` at an index `1 ≤ i < (p²−1)/2`; and the specified
valuation ring `A` lies over `p`. None is replaced or strengthened. Primality
and `p ≠ 2` give `p ≥ 3` and oddness. The coefficient's positive index is used
in good reduction; its stated upper bound remains part of the frozen
hypothesis but is not needed for this proof route. Semistability is used in
the multiplicative branch to make `c₄` a unit.

The two accepted dependencies actually invoked are precisely:

## The common kernel argument

It suffices to construct an additive homomorphism `f : V → M` into any
commutative additive group, with a nonzero value, satisfying `f(σv)=f(v)` for
`σ ∈ I`. Its additive kernel is a `ZMod p` submodule by the existing
`AddSubgroup.toZModSubmodule` construction. A nonzero value proves this
submodule is not all of `V`. Additivity and invariance give
`f(σv−v)=f(σv)−f(v)=0`, proving the required inclusion.

Because the image of `p` lies in the maximal ideal of `A`, its residue field
`k` has characteristic `p`. For every integer `n`, residue nonvanishing is
equivalent both to `IsUnit (n : A)` and to `p ∤ n`. This is the integer-unit
criterion used throughout. The frozen inertia subgroup is the image of the
inertia subgroup of the decomposition group. Unpacking membership therefore
provides a lift stabilizing `A` whose residue action is the identity. In
particular every `σ ∈ I` preserves integrality and fixes all residues.

## Good reduction: an integral torsion point and specialization

Suppose first that `p ∤ Δ(W)`. The discriminant is a unit in `A`, and the
reduced equation over `k` has nonzero discriminant.

We first prove an integral-root fact. A polynomial `f ∈ A[X]` with a unit
coefficient in positive degree has a root in `A`. Otherwise every root `r` of
its image over the algebraically closed field `K` is outside `A`, so `r⁻¹`
lies in the maximal ideal. Factor the image of `f` into its linear factors.
Rewrite each `X−r` as `−r(1−r⁻¹X)`. The product `g` of the latter factors
belongs to `A[X]` and reduces to 1. Comparing the constant coefficients gives
`f = f(0)g` over `A` by injectivity into `K`. The reduction of `f` is then
constant, contradicting its positive-degree unit coefficient. This proof
allows repeated roots and does not require a unit leading coefficient.

The ordinary alternative supplies such a coefficient for `W.preΨ' p`.
The integral-root fact supplies its root `x ∈ A`. Apply it also to the monic
quadratic
`Y²+(a₁x+a₃)Y−(x³+a₂x²+a₄x+a₆)` to obtain `y ∈ A` on the curve.
The characteristic-zero image of the integer discriminant is nonzero, so the
point `(x,y)` is nonsingular. The approved odd-division theorem, with `p ≥ 3`
and `p` odd, shows that this affine point is killed by `p`. Its reduced
coordinates are nonsingular because the reduced discriminant is nonzero;
its reduction is an affine point and hence is not the identity.

We now construct specialization directly from the pinned affine formulas.
For a point on the integral equation, `x ∈ A` if and only if `y ∈ A`. If only
one coordinate were integral, dividing the equation by the appropriate power
of the other coordinate and reducing would put 1 in the maximal ideal.
Send points with integral coordinates to their residue coordinates and all
other points, including the identity, to the identity. Nonsingularity of
finite reductions follows from the reduced discriminant. The integral
negation formula shows that this map respects negation.

Here is the additivity calculation, including coincident reductions. Consider
three affine intersections of a nonvertical secant or tangent with slope
`l`, counted with multiplicity. The pinned `addPolynomial_slope` and
`nonsingular_negAdd` identify the third intersection and its cubic
factorization.

If `l ∈ A`, write the line as `y=lx+n`. When `n ∈ A`, substitution gives a
monic cubic in `x`. None of its roots can be nonintegral: division by the
cube of such a root and reduction would give `1=0`. All three points are
therefore integral. Reduce the line equations and cubic factorization.
Comparing the quadratic coefficient gives the third x-coordinate in the
addition formula. If the first two reduced points coincide, differentiating
the factorization and using nonsingularity proves the tangent-slope formula.
Thus the three reduced points sum to zero. If `n` is not integral, none of
the three points is integral and all reduce to zero.

If `l ∉ A`, put `t=l⁻¹`, a nonunit, and write the line as `x=ty+n`. A
nonintegral `n` again gives three zero reductions. For integral `n`, the
substituted polynomial `f(Y)` has factorization
`−t³(Y−y₁)(Y−y₂)(Y−y₃)` and reduces to a monic quadratic. Its linear
coefficient after reduction is `a₁n+a₃`. The integral-root fact gives one
integral root. Division by its monic linear factor leaves a quotient whose
quadratic coefficient is a nonunit and whose linear coefficient is a unit,
so a second application gives another integral root. All three roots cannot
be integral, since then the formula for their sum would make `f`'s unit
quadratic coefficient a nonunit. Exactly two roots, counting multiplicity,
are integral. For these roots `b,d`, coefficient comparison yields
`f₁+f₂(b+d)−t³(b²+bd+d²)=0`. Reduction shows that their y-coordinates sum to
`−a₁n−a₃`, while both x-coordinates reduce to `n`. Their reductions are
therefore negatives; the third reduction is zero.

These chord identities prove additivity for affine pairs that are not
negatives. Zero and opposite pairs follow from preservation of zero and
negation. Thus specialization is an additive homomorphism even for tangency,
coincident reductions, and lines becoming vertical in the special fiber.
An element of `I` preserves the integral/nonintegral cases and fixes the
residue coordinates, so specialization is inertia invariant. Its restriction
to `V` has the nonzero value constructed above. The common kernel argument
settles good reduction.

## A valued field model for the specified inertia group

For the remaining case `p ∣ Δ(W)`, we construct the analytic fields rather
than assume a compatible embedding into a chosen p-adic field. The local
`tate_ambient` argument produces a complete discretely normed
characteristic-zero field `F`, an algebraically closed algebraic normed
extension `Ω/F`, and an injective ring map `ι : K → Ω` such that

`‖ιx‖ ≤ 1 ↔ x ∈ A`, and `‖ιx‖ < 1 ↔ x ∈ A.nonunits`.

Both norms are ultrametric. Every `σ ∈ I` extends to an `F`-automorphism `υ`
of `Ω` with `υ(ιx)=ι(σx)` and
`‖υz−z‖<1` whenever `‖z‖≤1`. The construction is as follows.

For the canonical valuation of `A`, a nonzero algebraic element `x` satisfies
`v(x)^n=v(a)` for some positive integer `n` and nonzero rational `a`.
Indeed, in a polynomial relation for `x`, a unique term of largest valuation
would give the sum that same nonzero valuation, a contradiction. Two terms
of equal largest valuation give the stated relation after division and
subtraction of their distinct exponents. Factoring powers of `p` from
integers and applying the integer-unit criterion shows that every nonzero
rational has value an integer power of `v(p)`.

Every nonzero value is consequently commensurable with `v(p)⁻¹>1`. If
`z^n=(v(p)⁻¹)^m`, assign `z` the logarithmic value `m/n`. Common powers prove
independence of choices, additivity on products, and strict order
preservation. Exponentiation produces a multiplicative order-preserving map
to the positive reals; send zero to zero. Composing with the original
valuation gives an ultrametric absolute value on `K` with exactly the two
asserted balls. The norm of `p` lies strictly between zero and one, and
rational norms are its integer powers. A decomposition automorphism
preserves the valuation ring and its units. Applying it to the power
relation proves that it preserves this absolute value.

Complete this normed field to `C`. Inversion preserves Cauchy filters bounded
away from zero, using
`x⁻¹−y⁻¹=x⁻¹(y−x)y⁻¹` and bounded inverse norms; the pinned uniform-field
completion then gives a field. Multiplicativity and the ultrametric
inequality extend by density and closedness. The completion maps extend an
isometric automorphism and its inverse to an isometric ring automorphism of
`C`. If such an automorphism comes from inertia, approximate an integral
`z ∈ C` by an element of `K` within distance less than 1. That approximant
is integral. Residue invariance there and isometry at both endpoints show
that the difference at `z` has norm less than 1.

We prove that `C` is algebraically closed. Approximate each monic polynomial
of positive degree `d` over `C` coefficientwise by monic degree-d polynomials
over the dense algebraically closed subfield `K`. Choose a common bound
`M ≥ 1` larger than the relevant coefficient norms. A root of any
approximant has norm at most `M`: beyond that bound the leading term would
be the unique term of largest norm. Choose coefficient errors smaller than
`δₙ=(2⁻ⁿ)^d/(4M^d)`. The ultrametric finite-sum bound controls the evaluation
error at a point of norm at most `M` by `δₙM^d`. Factoring the next monic
approximant shows that if its value at a current root has norm less than
`r^d`, some next root is at distance less than `r`; otherwise the product
of all distances would be at least `r^d`. This constructs successive roots
at distances less than `2⁻ⁿ`. The geometric Cauchy criterion and completeness
give a limit. The evaluation errors tend to zero, and explicit continuity
of the finite coefficient sum makes that limit a root of the original
polynomial. Hence every monic positive-degree polynomial has a root.

Let `F` be the topological closure in `C` of the rational subfield. It is a
closed subfield, hence complete. For a nonzero element of `F`, a sufficiently
close rational approximant has exactly its norm by ultrametricity; thus the
nonzero norms in `F` are integer powers of `‖p‖`. A continuous ring
automorphism fixes the rationals and therefore fixes `F` pointwise.
Let `Ω` be the relative algebraic closure of `F` in `C`. Since `C` is
algebraically closed, the pinned relative-closure theorem makes `Ω` an
algebraically closed algebraic extension of `F`. Its subfield norm gives
`NormedAlgebra F Ω`. The original copy of `K` lies in `Ω`, since each of
its elements is algebraic over the rationals and hence over `F`. Restrict
the extended inertia automorphisms to `Ω`. This establishes all the promised
ambient-field properties and every analytic hypothesis of the approved Tate
dependency. Classical choice supplies decidable equality where required.

## Constructing the Tate parameter

Semistability and `p ∣ Δ(W)` give `p ∤ c₄(W)`. On `W_F`, therefore,
`0<‖Δ‖<1` and `‖c₄‖=1`. Put `w=Δ/c₄³`, so `0<‖w‖<1`.
We construct `q ∈ F` with `‖q‖=‖w‖` for which the approved explicit Tate
curve has inverse j-invariant `w`.

Write

`s_k(q)=Σ_{d≥0}(d+1)^k q^{d+1}/(1−q^{d+1})`,
`P(q)=Π_{d≥0}(1−q^{d+1})²⁴`,
`A(q)=1+240s₃(q)`, and `U(q)=P(q)/A(q)³`.

For `0<‖q‖<1`, the approved Tate theorem gives convergence of these series
and product. At `q=0` convergence is immediate. In an ultrametric field
integers have norm at most 1; also `‖1−x‖=1` for `‖x‖<1`. Induction gives
`‖xⁿ−yⁿ‖≤‖x−y‖` for `‖x‖,‖y‖≤1`. Applying these estimates to each term,
then to finite sums/products and their limits, gives

`‖s_k(q)‖≤‖q‖`, `‖s_k(q)−s_k(r)‖≤‖q−r‖`,
`‖P(q)‖=1`, `‖P(q)−1‖≤‖q‖`, and `‖P(q)−P(r)‖≤‖q−r‖`.

For products the difference estimate follows by telescoping one factor at
a time, using that all factors have norm one. The same bounds hold for
`A`, with `‖A(q)‖=1`. Subtracting two quotients with unit denominators
then gives `‖U(q)‖=1`, `‖U(q)−1‖≤‖q‖`, and
`‖U(q)−U(r)‖≤‖q−r‖`.

Set `ρ=‖w‖`. On the closed ball of radius `ρ`, the map
`f(x)=w−x(U(x)−1)` preserves the ball and satisfies
`‖f(x)−f(y)‖≤ρ‖x−y‖`. These facts follow by expanding the difference as
`−((x−y)(U(x)−1)+y(U(x)−U(y)))` and using the bounds above.
Starting from zero, successive differences are bounded by `‖w‖ρⁿ`.
The geometric Cauchy criterion gives a limit `q` in this ball. The contraction
bound proves continuity along the iterates, so the limit is fixed. Thus
`qU(q)=w`, and the unit norm of `U(q)` gives `‖q‖=‖w‖`.

Let `T` be the explicit curve with coefficients
`(1,0,0,−5s₃(q),−(5s₃(q)+7s₅(q))/12)`.
The accepted invariant identities give
`T.c₄=A(q)` and `T.Δ=qP(q)`. Consequently
`T.Δ/T.c₄³=W_F.Δ/W_F.c₄³`, `‖T.c₄‖=1`, and
`‖T.Δ‖=‖W_F.Δ‖<1`. Also `q ≠ 0`, so the full approved
uniformization theorem applies to this same parameter.

## An inertia-equivariant isomorphism with this Tate curve

For either curve, `c₄³−c₆²=1728Δ`, `‖c₄‖=1`, and `‖Δ‖<1`
imply `‖c₆‖=1`. In particular all the c-invariants used as denominators
are nonzero. Put `a=W_F.c₄/T.c₄`, `b=W_F.c₆/T.c₆`. The equal inverse
j-invariants and the invariant identity imply `b²=a³`. Thus `d=b/a`
satisfies `d²T.c₄=W_F.c₄` and `d³T.c₆=W_F.c₆`.
Choose `u ∈ Ω` with `u²=d`. It is nonzero, and taking norms in
`u⁴T.c₄=W_F.c₄` shows that `‖u‖=1`.

The explicit variable-change parameters are

`r=(u²T.b₂−W_F.b₂)/12`,
`s=(uT.a₁−W_F.a₁)/2`,
`t=(u³T.a₃−W_F.a₃−rW_F.a₁)/2`.

Using the pinned invariant transformation identities, this change sends
`W_F` to `T`: it matches `a₁,a₃,b₂,c₄,c₆`, which determine all five
coefficients in characteristic zero. On points the inverse-direction
coordinate map is
`(x,y) ↦ (u²x+r, u³y+u²sx+t)` from `T(Ω)` to `W_F(Ω)`.
The implementation checks the equation directly. It proves this map
additive using the pinned point formulas: opposite points remain opposite;
secant and tangent slopes transform as `l ↦ ul+s`; substitution into
`addX` and `addY` gives compatibility with addition. The invertible
coordinate formulas give injectivity and surjectivity. Thus this is a
bijective additive homomorphism, not just a bijection of curve equations.

Since `p ≠ 2`, the integer-unit criterion gives `‖2‖=1` in `Ω`.
For an extended inertia automorphism `υ`, the element `d ∈ F` is fixed,
so `υu=u` or `υu=−u`. In the second case,
`‖υu−u‖=‖2u‖=1`, contrary to its residue-trivial action on the unit
`u`. Hence it fixes `u`, and the displayed formulas show that it fixes
`r,s,t`. The coordinate isomorphism is therefore equivariant for every
extended element of the original inertia group. This proves the needed
compatibility directly, without assuming a separate unramified splitting
extension.

## The Tate quotient and its descent to the original module

The accepted theorem gives a surjective additive homomorphism
`θ : Additive Ωˣ → T(Ω)` whose kernel is exactly the powers of `q`,
and which is equivariant under `Ω`-automorphisms fixing `F`.
The map `n ↦ qⁿ` on integers is injective: taking norms reduces equality
to equality of powers of a real number strictly between zero and one.
Algebraic closedness supplies a pth root of `q`, which is a unit since
`q ≠ 0`.

For `v ∈ T(Ω)[p]`, choose a lift `u ∈ Ωˣ`. Since `p v=0`, we have
`uᵖ=qᵐ` for a unique integer `m`. If another lift differs by `qᵏ`,
its exponent differs by `pk`, so `m mod p` is independent of the lift.
Products of lifts show that this defines an additive homomorphism
`b_T : T(Ω)[p] → ZMod p`. A pth root of `q` has exponent 1, so this
homomorphism has a nonzero value. An automorphism fixing `q` and commuting
with `θ` sends the same exponent relation to itself; hence `b_T` is
invariant under all automorphisms over `F`. Transfer it across the
inertia-equivariant additive isomorphism just constructed to obtain a
nonzero inertia-invariant homomorphism on `W_F(Ω)[p]`.

We finally descend this homomorphism along the specified `ι : K → Ω`.
First, every p-torsion point of the base change of `E` to `Ω` comes from
a p-torsion point over `K`. For an affine point, the approved odd-division
theorem says that its x-coordinate is a root of the image of `E.preΨ' p`.
That polynomial is nonzero because its leading coefficient is `p` in
characteristic zero. It splits over `K`, so a root in the extension is the
image of a root in `K`. After descending x, the monic y-coordinate quadratic
also splits over `K`, so y descends. Nonsingularity descends by injectivity
of the field map. Injectivity of the additive point base-change map shows
that the preimage is killed by `p`; zero is handled separately.

The two base changes from the original integer equation to `Ω` agree
coefficientwise. The implementation explicitly identifies their point groups
and composes the point map induced by `ι` with that identification. The
resulting map from the exact frozen `V` to `W_F(Ω)[p]` is surjective by
the preceding argument. Pulling back the nonzero quotient therefore keeps
a nonzero value. For `σ ∈ I`, choose its extension `υ`. The identity
`υ(ιx)=ι(σx)` on coordinates proves compatibility of the two frozen
Galois actions. The quotient's invariance under `υ` consequently gives
invariance under `σ` on the original `V`. Apply the common kernel argument.
This finishes the multiplicative case and the theorem.
